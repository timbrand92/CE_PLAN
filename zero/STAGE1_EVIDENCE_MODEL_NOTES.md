## What it teaches about drawings (useful, matches what I saw on the real sheet)

1. **A drawing object is not five unrelated things.** A pipe's line, its label, its level texts, its flow arrow, its schedule row and its calculation row are all showings of one pipe.
2. **Connection is not nearness.** Two lines crossing do not prove a connection; a note near a beam is not that beam's label unless something ties them.
3. **A pit keeps its identity when its tag changes.** Renaming P5 must not make a new pit or break its pipes.
4. **Not everything in the drawing is live.** Model space holds parked template blanks and superseded bits beside the real design (seen on the real job: blank "IL _____" forms sit in the drawing).
5. **Things that drain are more than pits and pipes**: downpipes, inspection openings and overflows are points on the network but do not belong in the pit schedule; tanks are storage, not pits; spoon drains, trench grates and agricultural drains are line drains with somewhere to fall to; roofs, driveways and gardens are surfaces that drain to something.
6. **Every value has a source and a certainty**: survey, architect, council, calculation, read off the drawing, assumed, confirmed by the engineer. This fits Tim's 16 Nov point that design starts on assumptions that later change.
7. **Documents disagree in real jobs.** The 30-job study found spreadsheets, exported calculations, schedules and plans carrying different values for the same thing. The weakest areas were calculation source and plan / schedule / detail agreement.
8. **Councils differ in detail**: one council alone had at least 27 different note blocks depending on the job's conditions.
9. **Sheet grammar repeats**: main plan dominant, schedule and criteria beside it, job-specific details, fixed notes and full legend, title and approval zone at the edge. All 30 jobs followed it.
10. **Structural coverage so far**: mainly a new two-storey single dwelling for one repeat client, studied part-way; other job kinds and systems (waffle slab, alterations, townhouses, warehouses, pools, basements) had little or no review.

## My honest reading of why the old approach felt heavy (to test with Tim, not a decision)

1. **The core idea is simple and sound**: one fact kept in one place, things know what they connect to, and every showing of a fact reads from that one place.
2. **The old model wrapped it in a lot of layers**: about 12 kinds of record, 7 states of certainty, separate approval states for engineer / issue / council / construction, and 146 open questions before design-changing work could start. It also describes about 90 command names.
3. **Much of the evidence work stopped at "partial"** and at planning cards; the knowledge register itself says command-by-command checking in real AutoCAD was not done.
4. **For the zero plan**, the lesson to take is the short list above. How many of the layers are really needed for a house or units job done in under half a day is a Stage 2 and Stage 3 question for Tim.
