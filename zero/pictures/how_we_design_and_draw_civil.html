2. How the engineer designs it

One document decides almost everything: the council's discharge letter (with the planning permit). It says whether there is detention, which method, which storms, and whether a pump is allowed.

The questions, in the order they are answered

  1. Where does it drain to? Kerb, council pit or pipe, easement drain, a new outfall in the road, or soak on site.
  2. Is the flow restricted? No figure in the letter: no detention sum (plain house). A figure, or a method: the detention chain below.
  3. Can it fall there by gravity? If not: pump station (and some councils forbid pumping to the kerb).
  4. Is water quality asked for? STORM report: tank reuse litres, roof area to each tank, raingarden.
  5. Then the layout: pits, pipes, tanks, levels, tuned until every check passes.

The detention chain (house or units)

AreasSite, impervious (roofs + paving), pervious.
Coefficient0.9 impervious, 0.3 or 0.35 pervious, or the council's fixed figure.
Permitted dischargeGiven by council, or coefficient x 1-in-5 rain x area / 3600, or OSD4W.
Volume requiredBoyd's table (largest of 5 to 30 minute storms), or OSD4W, or given by council.
Storage providedEach pipe + each pit + each tank's detention share. Must be at least the volume required.
OrificeSize tried until flow is just under the permitted discharge.

Worked from a real two-unit job: site 650, impervious 376, coefficient 0.65. Council gives 6.00 l/s. Boyd's: 1.66 m³ required. Two tanks at 1.00 = 2.00 m³. Orifice 69 at 380 plate height: 5.84 l/s, under 6.00.

The numbers the engineer tunes

Pipe lengths and sizes, tank split (detention / reuse), top water level, baffle height, orifice. On one job all three landed exactly on that council's limits: 60% underground, 65 orifice, total equal to required.

3. Every civil job kind found, and what makes each different

13 real jobs across 9 councils. Each card: what triggers it, the extra sum, what extra lands on the sheet.

A. House, no detention

Letter gives discharge options and no flow figure. Pits, 100 pipes at 1 in 100, inverts worked back from the council pit, a reuse tank. No criteria box. No sums were in the folder.

B. House with detention

One line in the letter ("restricted to 5 l/s") triggers the whole chain. Seen: 9 m of 225 detention pipe + two pits + 1,000 l of the tank = 1.52 against 1.49.

C. Two units, tanks only

All detention in the tanks, one orifice pit. Smallest detention sheet: two pits in the schedule.

D. Units with detention pipes

Storage is a list by name: each pipe, each pit, each tank. Twin 225 or single 300 pipes at flat grades (1 in 150 to 1 in 200). Share underground must meet the council's minimum.

E. OSD4W council

The program replaces Boyd's with the council's own settings. Everything after is the same. One job was done in Boyd's first (3.27 m³), then redone in OSD4W (4.41 m³).

F. Council supplies the figures

Office emails site and impervious areas; council replies with discharge and storage (3 to 19 days seen). No detention sum by the office, only the storage list and orifice.

G. Pumped (low site)

Pump rate = permitted discharge. Wet well = largest of: 2-hour storm less 30 minutes pumping, 1% of catchment, 3 m³. Seven named levels stacked down the well. Adds a pump box, well section, rising main.

H. Soakage

Soil test gives the soak rate. For every storm from 5 minutes to 7 days: water in less water soaking out. Adds module grid, silt pit, sections. One job's pit grew from 6.9 to 16.2 m³ after council review.

I. Raingarden / STORM

Area comes from the STORM report (1 m² treating 34 to 50 m² of paving). Adds outline, section and filter layers. How the area is chosen is not in the files.

J. Outfall in the road

A second sheet: outfall plan, concrete pipe at 1 in 200, long section (size, grade, flow, velocity, invert, surface, chainage), services crossing section, council standard drawings.

K. Industrial

Coefficient 0.9, 1 in 20 storm, bigger pipes, modular underground tank, class D lids, two or three sheets. Some permits ask for a 25% yearly runoff cut by reuse (month-by-month tank balance).

L. Basement

A separate basement drainage plan, tanked basement, pump station, strip drains and floor wastes, "no groundwater to the kerb". The mark-up seen was mostly levels (about 30 written on).

"Civil site job"

Your folders show no separate product. Pavement (driveway slab, joints, saw cuts, mesh) always rides on the "stormwater drainage and pavement plan". Industrial and basement jobs are the same sheet with more pavement and levels. Not found at all: earthworks or retaining wall design drawn by civil.

4. How the drafter draws it: one pipe run, taken apart

This is a real pipe between two pits as it sits in your finished drawings. Count the separate objects.

  1. 1The blue band: a polyline with its width set to the pipe diameter (100, 150, 225, 300), layer STORMWATER PIPE, dashed. Charged pipes are the same on a red layer.
  2. 2The two pipe walls: a separate double line (multiline) on layer DRAINAGE, scaled to the diameter, drawn over the band.
  3. 3The label: loose masked text, 2.5 mm, turned to the pipe, about 0.75 to 2 mm clear of the pipe edge, either side. Wording: size, material, class, "AT 1 IN" grade.
  4. 4The flow arrow: a separate 7.5 mm leader beside the pipe. Half point the way the pipe was drawn, half the other way.
  5. 5Invert levels: loose 1.8 mm red text, one at each pipe end, about 0.5 mm clear of the pipe.
  6. 6Cover level: loose 2 mm text at 45 degrees near the pit.
  7. 7The pit: one block, "Stormwater Pit", with 16 looks (grated, junction, sizes, detention, pump station). It carries no size, number or level. A plastic pit is the small grated look with loose text "PP" beside it.
  8. 8The pit tag: a circle block with one typed value (P1). It is a re-used structural beam tag.

13 separate objects for this one run with its two pits. Nothing ties any of them together: no link from label to pipe, level to pit, or tag to pit. A detention pipe is just a wider band; only its label says "detention".

The tables are not tables

  1. Pit schedule: a block of ruled lines that stretches to add rows, with every cell a separate loose text laid on top (26 loose texts for a two-pit job).
  2. Detention criteria box: a rectangle, a heading, and one block of text lined up with tabs.
  3. Title block: no fill-in fields. Job values are edited inside the block. Each revision row is six loose texts, copied up and retyped.
  4. Issue stamp: four stamps stacked in one spot on four layers; three are frozen.
  5. Details: drawn five times real size so they read 1:20 through the 1:100 window, with every dimension typed over by hand (measures 750, reads "150"). The orifice pit and tank details carry this job's numbers typed inside the block.

5. How the sheet is put together

Blue = drawn for this job. Green = typed from the design. Grey = the fixed frame, the same on every job.

What I measured

  1. Everything is in model space, laid out inside a non-printing frame as if it were a giant A1 sheet 100 times bigger. Same position on every job.
  2. One locked 1:100 viewport. Paper space holds only the title block.
  3. A second sheet is a second frame 100 m to the right.
  4. The background is one office-cleaned file attached two or three times and clipped (site, ground, part plans). Boundary, bearings, bench mark, survey levels and street name print from it; they are not in the job drawing.
  5. Nothing scales itself. All text is fixed height (250 = 2.5 mm).
  6. Colour decides the pen. Colours 1 to 9 print black or grey at set thicknesses; almost every other colour prints in colour. That is why the sheet says "print in full colour".
  7. Layer names by pen: 18, 20, 25, 35, 50, 70 + CONT / TEXT / HIDDEN. With today's pen table the "18" layers print 0.13 mm and the "70" layers 0.40 mm.
  8. Copy-and-fill is the method. The template parks about 600 sample objects beside the sheet ("IL _____", every symbol, sample pipes with labels). A finished job still carries about 400 of them.

6. Where it goes wrong today (all seen in your real files)

On issued sheets

  1. Sheet shows 32.53 l/s and a 100 orifice; the sums say 34.53 and 121.
  2. Raingarden 3.5 m² on the sheet; 1.0 m² in the STORM report.
  3. Council pipe 225 on the sheet; 375 in the letter.
  4. Orifice 55 against 56; tank split differing between plan and detail on one stamped sheet.
  5. A schedule with two rows both "P1".

In the workbooks

  1. A slipped cell gives 0 l/s and still prints OKAY.
  2. A pit labelled 600 x 600 worked as 0.6 x 0.9 (three jobs).
  3. The newer detention workbook stops at five pits.
  4. "Head" printed on the sheet (380) is the plate height; the sum uses 345.
  5. Every job folder is seeded with about 15 stale sum files carrying other jobs' numbers.

In the office rules

  1. Pervious coefficient: 0.3 in the guidebook, 0.35 in the Boyd's sheets.
  2. Pipe cover: four different minimums across four documents.
  3. Step irons: over 900, over 1,000 or over 1,200 deep.
  4. Council tabs copied without being changed; one council listed under two methods. One job was designed in Boyd's, then redone in OSD4W.
My correction. Earlier today I told you a 69 orifice at 380 head gives 5.99 l/s, matching the sheet's 6.00. Your job's own sum uses 345 head and gives 5.84. I used the plate height as the head. The sheet prints the plate height under the word "head", which is how I slipped.

The pattern

The design is a small set of facts: the council's figures, the areas, and the network (pits, pipes, tanks with their levels and sizes). Today those facts are typed into a workbook, written on a mark-up, typed onto the plan, typed again into the schedule, the criteria box and the details, and nothing connects the copies. Every mismatch above is one copy changing without the others.
