**What was read:** the words only. The 33 pictures in it (the workflow figure, the spreadsheet extracts, the OSD4W screen, the mark-up example, pipe and cover figures, the council map) were NOT seen. They still need to be looked at.
**Status:** notes from the document, not yet confirmed by Tim as how the office really works today.

## The job in the guidebook's order

1. **Set up and gather.** A job folder by year. Must have before starting: the legal point of discharge location, the architect's plans and CAD files, the feature survey with site levels. May come later: planning permit, site investigation of the discharge point, endorsed plans, landscape plans, arborist report, soil report, sustainability assessment.
2. **Understand the requirement.** Job kind (single house, units, commercial, industrial), which council (and Melbourne Water), and the site: slope, discharge point, existing and proposed conditions, council assets, other services, flood levels, neighbours' footings, trees to keep. The council's discharge document and the planning permit say whether detention is needed.
3. **Computations.** Two numbers come first: the permitted site discharge and the on-site detention volume. Three ways to get them, chosen by council:
   - the council gives them after the office emails the site area and impervious area;
   - Boyd's method in an office spreadsheet (rational method, rainfall from the Bureau of Meteorology site for the job's location);
   - the Swinburne method in the OSD4W program.
   The two calculation methods can give different answers, so the council's accepted method is confirmed first.
4. **Schematic design / mark-up.** Pits, detention pipes and water tanks are laid out by trial and error on the A1 plans, usually near the discharge point at the low end of the site. Things to work around: slope, tree protection zones, neighbours' footings, sewer ties (1 m clear), paving, landscaping, the architect's intent. Office spreadsheets size the detention pipes and pits, the orifice, pumps and soakage.
5. **Water tanks and water quality.** Tank reuse volume comes from the STORM report or sustainability assessment or the permit; how much of a tank may count as detention is set by each council.
6. **Drafting.** The design is marked up on an A1 sheet, then drafted. The engineer checks thoroughly after the first draft, and against the council's checklist.
7. **Council submission.** Each council has its own way in (online portal or email), forms, fees and a list of things it commonly asks for.

## Elements the guidebook says appear on the drawings (start of the element list)

1. On-site detention criteria table
2. Pit schedule (all pits scheduled, plastic pits included)
3. Water tank detail, including the orifice; tank size and dimensions
4. Charged system notes
5. Water tank installation diagram
6. Pipe bedding details
7. Junction pit detail
8. Services penetration detail
9. Paving details, permeable paving detail, driveway pavement cross-section with crossfall (some councils)
10. Orifice pit detail (two chambers and a baffle wall; one council uses a single chamber with an orifice plate), plan and section of the baffle pit (some councils)
11. Angle of repose detail
12. Council-specific notes and council standard drainage notes
13. Title dimensions, street names, the temporary bench mark, crossover locations and widths, the discharge note
14. Roof hatch for the area going to the water tank, with a hatch legend giving roof area in square metres per dwelling
15. Overland flow path arrows and legend
16. Dimension notes for pipes and pits at least 1 m from the splay
17. Spot levels at corners and near pits
18. Inspection openings at changes of direction
19. Pipe notes (sewer quality; 150 dia under garages)
20. Vehicle crossings removed and reinstated, pedestrian islands
21. Outfall drain long section where a council asks (one council: horizontal 1:250, vertical 1:25)
22. Property connection and new junction pit details to council standard drawings (some councils)
23. Pump system and soakage system details where used

## Rules of thumb in the guidebook (values to be confirmed with Tim before any use)

1. Minimum pipe fall 1 in 100 for gravity pipes; some councils allow 1 in 200 for detention pipes 225 and larger.
2. Minimum underground pipe 100 dia.
3. Pit size goes by depth: 350 x 350 to 450 deep; 450 x 450 to 600; 600 x 600 to 900; 600 x 900 to 1200 with step irons; 900 x 900 deeper than 1200.
4. Orifice pit: at least two 600 x 600 chambers, 100 clear between lid and top of baffle wall. Minimum orifice size differs by council.
5. Share of detention that must be underground differs by council (50%, 60%, all).
6. No detention pipes under dwellings or garages (rear discharge under decking is the exception).
7. Charged lines to tanks: at least 300 head, 135 degree bends with inspection openings.
8. The overflow of the detention system sits lower than every pit cover on the site.

## What this means for planning (my reading, to be tested with Tim)

1. **The council drives almost everything**: method, storm events, minimum sizes, required details, notes, submission. Council requirements look like data that changes over time.
2. **The design is done before drafting, on paper and in spreadsheets**, then drawn. The same numbers live in the spreadsheet, the mark-up and the drawing, which is where "drawing does not match" can come from.
3. **Design often starts on assumptions** (Tim, 16 Nov 2026, see the interview record). Items the guidebook itself marks as "may not be available at the start" are the likely ones.
