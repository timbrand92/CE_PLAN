End of the system: two new symbols

The office has no symbol for either of these. Each picture is a plan view with the property at the top and the road at the bottom, drawn as it would plot at 1:100: black on white, thin and medium lines only.

Big picture: about 4 times plot size. Small picture beside it: true plot size at 1:100 (about 9 mm square for a pit), if your screen is at 100% zoom.

(a) Existing council pit in the street

The site pipe connects to a pit that is already there, for example a side-entry pit or a junction pit. It must read as existing, not new, and carry a short tag.

A
Recommended

Why: dashed already says "existing" before anyone reads the tag, it stays clear at 1:100, and it is the simplest block (one dashed line type, no fills).

True plot size, 1:100

What it says to the builderThe pipe connects into a pit that already exists. Do not build a pit here.
Drawn in AutoCAD asOne block: dashed square with a diagonal, and a tag attribute (ExP or SEP).
B

Square with a cross, and the letters EX in the tag.

True plot size, 1:100

What it says to the builderA pit marked EX is existing. Connect to it; do not build it.
Drawn in AutoCAD asOne block: solid square with a cross, and a tag attribute that starts with EX.
C

Double dashed outline: a dashed square inside a dashed square.

True plot size, 1:100

What it says to the builderThe pipe connects into an existing pit. Nothing new is built here.
Drawn in AutoCAD asOne block: two dashed squares, and a tag attribute (ExP or SEP). The gap between them gets crowded at 1:100.
D

Half-filled square: one corner to corner half is solid black.

True plot size, 1:100

What it says to the builderThis pit is already in the street. Connect to it.
Drawn in AutoCAD asOne block: square plus a solid half (a hatch), and a tag attribute (ExP or SEP). Reads bold on a busy plan.
Does not plot: a CE marker on a no-plot layer still marks where the system ends (ruled earlier).

(b) Kerb outlet

The site pipe discharges through the kerb to the channel (a kerb adaptor). Each picture shows the kerb and channel in plan, with the pipe arriving from the property side. The three lines are back of kerb (thin), face of kerb (medium) and the edge of the channel (thin).

A
Recommended

Why: one solid shape that points at the road, so it shows the flow direction by itself and needs no extra arrow. The easiest block to read at 2.5 mm text size.

True plot size, 1:100

What it says to the builderThe pipe finishes at the kerb here and the water leaves toward the road.
Drawn in AutoCAD asOne block: small filled triangle, inserted on the face of the kerb and turned to point at the road.
B

Short solid bar across the pipe end, with an arrow to the channel.

True plot size, 1:100

What it says to the builderThe pipe ends at a bar on the kerb face; flow runs out to the channel.
Drawn in AutoCAD asOne block: solid bar plus an arrow, inserted on the kerb face and turned to the road.
C

Half circle on the kerb, round side toward the road.

True plot size, 1:100

What it says to the builderThe pipe discharges through the kerb at this half circle.
Drawn in AutoCAD asOne block: half circle on the kerb face, no arrow. Direction comes only from which way the round side points.
D

Small rectangle the width of the kerb, with a flow arrow.

True plot size, 1:100

What it says to the builderThe pipe goes through the kerb at this opening and flows out to the channel.
Drawn in AutoCAD asOne block: open rectangle the width of the kerb plus an arrow. Its size depends on the kerb type drawn, so it may need stretching.
Does not plot: a CE marker on a no-plot layer still marks where the system ends (ruled earlier).

Tags (ExP, EX SEP) and symbol sizes are drawing proposals for Tim to choose between. Nothing here is built into the tool.
